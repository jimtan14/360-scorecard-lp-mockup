Scoring breakdown

Freshness

Whether content is updated, datestamped, and signals recency — a major weighting factor in AI answer engines.

How it’s scored: Scored from datePublished/dateModified coverage, % of pages updated in the last 12 months, datestamp visibility in body copy, and statistic-currency.

92/100
Crawlability

Whether AI agents can actually fetch, render, and parse your pages. Includes robots.txt, sitemap, status codes, and JS rendering.

How it’s scored: Scored from sitemap coverage, % of pages returning 200, robots.txt allowlist for AI bots, and HTML-first vs JS-only render.

72/100
Entity Signals

How clearly the brand is represented as a named entity across the web — Wikipedia, Crunchbase, knowledge panels, structured author bios.

How it’s scored: Scored from Wikipedia presence, knowledge-panel detection, sameAs link count, author-entity schema, and brand mention density on third-party properties.

60/100
Schema Coverage

How well your pages declare structured data (JSON-LD) so engines can extract entities, FAQ, products, and articles cleanly.

How it’s scored: Scored from % of sampled pages with valid JSON-LD, schema-type diversity (Article, Product, FAQPage, HowTo, Organization), and required-field completeness.

55/100
Citation Worthiness

Whether your content is the kind of thing AI engines pull as a source — original data, expert analysis, definitive guides, structured comparisons.

How it’s scored: Scored from original-data presence, citation density (links to primary sources), comparison/listicle structures, and observed AI cite rate vs. category baseline.

78/100
Answerable Format

Whether content is structured as direct answers — H2 questions, lists, tables, definitions — that AI can lift into responses.

How it’s scored: Scored from H2-as-question rate, presence of TL;DR / key-takeaways blocks, table+list density, and average answer length under 300 words.

70/100
Offsite Readiness

Whether the brand is present on the third-party properties LLMs actually trust — Wikipedia, G2, Capterra, editorial sites, communities. 85% of brand mentions in AI come from offsite, so a brand absent here is invisible to most AI answers.

How it’s scored: Scored from named third-party properties citing the brand, competitor offsite citation share, original-research distribution potential, review-site coverage, and community presence (Reddit / Quora / Stack Overflow).

Strategic brief

Hex is losing its own category on the searches buyers use most.

When buyers compare notebook tools, they ask Google and ChatGPT first. Deepnote already owns those answers. They’ve built 30+ comparison pages and rank #1 for “hex vs deepnote,” “hex vs jupyter,” and “hex vs colab.”

We tested 7 obvious Hex comparison URLs — /compare/hex-vs-deepnote, /compare/hex-vs-mode, /compare/hex-vs-databricks, and four others. Every one returned a 404. Only two comparison pages exist today (/compare/sigma-vs-hex and /compare/looker-vs-hex), and both rank well when they’re live. The pages buyers are searching for simply don’t exist.

The structural picture makes it worse. Across our 16-page sample of hex.tech, 0% have a TL;DR summary at the top — the block AI engines copy into their answers. 25% have FAQ-style content, but 0% use the markup AI engines need to read it. When AI pulls from a Hex page, it rarely names Hex.

Offsite is the biggest gap. Across 15,157 third-party citations AI engines read for this category, Hex shows up in 0%. Reddit (2,792 citations), YouTube (1,204), Wikipedia (1,009), and Medium (983) drive the answers, and Hex isn’t named in any of them.

For category searches like “data visualization tool” (2,900/mo), “ai agent for data analysis,” and “snowflake notebook,” Deepnote, Julius, and Databricks own the AI answer. Hex doesn’t appear.

The bright spot: when Hex IS named in AI answers (“hex vs deepnote,” “hex alternatives,” “agentic analytics”), the citation leans on third-party sources. The brand voice works when it shows up — there just isn’t enough of it out there.
